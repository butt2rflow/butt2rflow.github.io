    Investing in Physical AI (2) — Value Shifts Seen Through One Joint: Reducers, Motors and ETFs¶

    Investing in Physical AI · Part 2. Part 1, "Investing in Physical AI" argued that "there's no pure play, and the value is moving." This part looks at that movement at the level of one humanoid joint. It's information, not investment advice.

    Part 1 called reducer makers the real picks and shovels, since "whoever wins, the joints all come from here." That was only half right; the other half comes with a condition. Depending on how the joint is designed, the money can go to the reducer, or it can move to motors and magnets. This post draws that condition out in pictures and checks how much of that logic the ETFs you can actually buy really capture.

    The 30-second version¶

    • The actuator that moves one robot joint is made of a motor, a reducer, sensors and a driver. Humanoids have several times more joints than industrial arms, so mass production would sharply raise actuator demand; that's the basic thesis.
    • Design is the key. With a high reduction ratio (how many motor turns make one joint turn), value concentrates in the precision reducer; with QDD (quasi-direct drive), it moves to motors, rare-earth magnets and drivers. One industry analysis sees different designs mixed across joints.
    • Demand is becoming real, but the bottlenecks are the hands, the supply chain, and the rare-earth risk that grows as value moves to motors.
    • Four US-listed robot ETFs (KOID, ROBO, BOTZ, BOTT) have similar names but different contents.

    Take apart one joint¶

    Which of the four parts captures the value is this post's question.

    One actuator per joint means actuators sell in proportion to humanoids. Keep in mind, though, that Tesla's production targets for its humanoid, Optimus, have slipped more than once.

    Design sets the direction of the money¶

    Opinions split here. A Korean blog analysis argues that more humanoids benefit the precision reducer makers. A YouTube engineering channel, on the other hand, argues that today's humanoids want flexible, agile motion more than precision, so QDD, which relies on a strong motor instead of an expensive reducer, has the edge. The two claims collide head-on.

    Neither side has won yet. High-ratio designs suit industrial arms and precise wrists and hands, while QDD or linear designs suit legs that walk, run and take impacts, so both are mixed within one robot. For investors this means: reducer makers can post earnings on the industrial-robot recovery alone, but the humanoid premium in their share prices is exposed to QDD adoption and cheap Chinese competition.

    Who makes what¶

    Precision reducers have long been an oligopoly of two Japanese companies. Harmonic Drive in small strain-wave reducers and Nabtesco in large cycloidal (RV) reducers are commonly cited as the share leaders. How much hope their share prices carry differs a lot: at the time of research (2026-09-28), forward 12-month P/E (the share price as a multiple of the next year's earnings) was in the 70s for Harmonic Drive and in the low 20s for Nabtesco. Nabtesco's business is spread across ships, rail and more, so less humanoid hope is priced in. Both have US over-the-counter tickers, but they barely trade; most trading happens in Tokyo.

    The biggest risk is the low-price chase from Chinese makers such as Leaderdrive. A stronger yen would weigh on Japanese exporters, and high interest rates on factory capex.

    On the QDD side, value moves to motors and magnets. The US-listed names in this layer carry very different amounts of hope too, just like the reducer makers. Figures are as of the 2026-09-29 close.

    • Allient (ALNT, Nasdaq): a small cap (about $1.9B market cap) that offers frameless and axial-flux motors for humanoids. The share price has roughly 2.5x'd in a year and sits near its 52-week high, at a forward P/E in the mid-30s. Being small, one order moves it a lot, and a lot of hope is already in the price.
    • Regal Rexnord (RRX, NYSE): its Kollmorgen unit supplies frameless servo motors for robot joints. Cheap at about 13x forward earnings, but robotics is a small slice of the whole company, so the humanoid effect is diluted. The shares are about 37% below their 52-week high.
    • MP Materials (MP, NYSE): the only mine-to-magnet operation in the West. It is still loss-making, so P/E comparisons do not work well (forward P/E above 100), and the shares are about 55% below their 52-week high, swinging hard on US-China news.

    China's Unitree, well known for QDD joints, listed on Shanghai's STAR Market in August 2026 (688836), but foreign retail investors can hardly buy it directly. In the US, Agility Robotics is heading for a year-end listing through a SPAC merger (expected ticker AGLT), and in Korea, Robotis (DYNAMIXEL-Q) and Higen RNM (developing QDD drive modules) are the names mentioned on the QDD side.

    Demand is becoming real; the hands are the bottleneck¶

    Contrary to the objection that "humanoids are still far off," Tesla's Optimus output really is rising. But the hands are still assembled by people, and motors and precision gears rely heavily on outside suppliers, many in China. As component demand becomes real, a new question opens up: which suppliers can hold quality in mass production.

    Rare earths, the weak point on the motor side¶

    In the QDD thesis, "who makes the motors" comes with a second question: "who makes magnets outside China?" That even the company held up as the alternative landed on China's export-control list shows how real this supply risk is.

    What you can actually buy: four ETFs¶

    ETFs are a way to hold a basket of stocks instead of picking component makers one by one. Similar names, though, hide quite different contents.

    Two facts not in the table: BOTT is small and had the weakest return of the four this year (+0.5% YTD as of 2026-09-25). And KOID and ROBO, which hold both Japanese reducer makers, do so at a combined weight of about 3–4%, so these ETFs are a weak bet on the reducer thesis.

    Holding two ETFs together gives more or less diversification depending on their overlap. ROBO and BOTZ overlap heavily because both hold large robotics and automation companies such as FANUC, Keyence, Yaskawa, ABB and NVIDIA. BOTT is over half Korean and Chinese small-cap robot stocks, entirely different in character from the others, which also brings that market's risk with it.

    Where, and in which account¶

    General information under the rules in effect as of September 2026. Taxes depend on your situation, so always check separately.

    • Korean investors buying US ETFs directly: use an overseas-stock account. Gains are taxed at 22% after an annual ₩2.5 million deduction, and distributions have 15% US withholding (tax taken at payment).
    • Korea-listed Physical AI ETFs: they can go into ISA and pension accounts, but many hold a fixed weight of around 25% in a single stock, Hyundai Motor, so check the single-stock concentration.
    • Canadian investors: US withholding on distributions is waived in an RRSP but not in a TFSA.

    What to watch¶

    Humanoid news moves share prices first, but what drives component makers' earnings right now is still capex at car and electronics plants. Industry commentary generally treats the industrial-robot cycle as the base for earnings, and the humanoid story as upside on top of it.

    Summary¶

    Question Answer
    Why actuators One per joint, and humanoids have several times more joints
    Where value goes High-ratio designs: reducers. QDD: motors, magnets, drivers. Mixed across joints
    Reducer makers Industrial recovery is the floor; the humanoid premium is exposed to QDD and cheap Chinese rivals
    QDD-side stocks ALNT (hope priced in), RRX (cheap but diluted), MP (loss-making, politics). Unitree is hard for foreign retail to buy
    Bottlenecks Hand assembly, reliance on outside suppliers, rare earths
    ETFs KOID (parts), ROBO (broad), BOTZ (large caps), BOTT (robotics pure-plays, small caps). ROBO+BOTZ overlap heavily
    What to watch Order backlog, R&D share, manufacturing PMI, auto capex

    If Part 1's conclusion was "the value is moving," Part 2's is this: joint design decides the direction. Not knowing yet which design wins is itself the risk in any thesis that depends on one.

    Glossary¶

    • Actuator: the module that moves a robot joint: motor, reducer, sensors and driver
    • Reducer: a precision part that turns a motor's fast spin into slow, strong rotation
    • Reduction ratio: how many motor turns make one joint turn; higher means stronger and slower
    • Strain-wave (harmonic) · cycloidal (RV) · planetary reducers: small and backlash-free · large and rigid · cheap and efficient
    • QDD (quasi-direct drive): a joint design with a low reduction ratio that gets its force mainly from a strong motor
    • Backlash: the play between gears; large backlash makes precise gripping or stopping hard
    • Rare-earth permanent magnet: the magnet in strong motors; production is concentrated in China
    • Frameless motor: a motor supplied as bare rotor and stator, with no housing or shaft, so it can be built straight into a joint. Common in QDD joints
    • SPAC: a shell company created only to list, which then merges with a private company to take it public
    • P/E (price-to-earnings): share price divided by earnings per share; higher means more future hope priced in (simplified)
    • Over-the-counter (OTC): trading outside a regular exchange; volume is often thin
    • Expense ratio: the annual cost of holding an ETF, as a share of assets
    • Market-cap weighting: giving bigger companies bigger weights
    • Holdings overlap: the weight of stocks two ETFs hold in common; the higher it is, the less diversification from buying both
    • Distribution · withholding: income an ETF pays out · tax deducted when it's paid
    • Order backlog: orders received but not yet delivered; a preview of future revenue
    • Manufacturing PMI: a business-cycle index built from surveys of factory purchasing managers; above 50 means expansion
